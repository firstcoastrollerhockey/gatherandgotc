How to Plan a Multi-Generational Family Vacation Everyone Will Love

October 2024 · 7 min read · By Gather & Go Travel Co.

← Back to Tips & Guides

Multi-generational trips — vacations that bring together grandparents, parents, and kids (and sometimes everyone in between) — are some of the most meaningful travel experiences a family can have. They're also some of the most complicated to plan.

Getting a four-year-old, a fourteen-year-old, two parents, and two grandparents to all have a genuinely great time in the same destination at the same time takes some real thought. But it's absolutely doable, and when it works, it creates the kind of shared family memories that people talk about for decades.

I've helped plan a lot of these trips. Here's what I've learned.

Start With Honest Conversations

The planning process for a multi-generational trip needs to start with a real conversation about what everyone actually wants — and especially about what the limitations are. This sounds obvious, but it's where a lot of these trips go wrong.

Grandparents may not be able to walk as much as you'd expect. They might need accessible transportation options, air conditioning, and more rest time than the rest of the group. They may have dietary restrictions or medical needs that affect destination and restaurant choices. They may be on a fixed income and the budget question is more delicate than it is for other members of the group.

Have these conversations before you start booking. Find out what grandparents can and can't do comfortably. Find out what the teenagers actually want (because a 15-year-old who spends the whole trip unhappy will affect everyone else). Find out whether there are any medical or mobility considerations you need to plan around.

The more honestly everyone communicates upfront, the better trip you'll plan.

Choose a Destination That Actually Works for Everyone

This is the most important decision you'll make. Not every destination is equally well-suited for multi-generational travel, and choosing the wrong one creates problems that no amount of planning can fix.

Walt Walt Disney World® Resort

Walt Disney World® Resort is genuinely one of the best multi-generational destinations in the world, and it's not by accident. Disney has thought deeply about accessibility — wheelchair rentals, accessible transportation throughout the property, no-wait boarding for guests with mobility limitations (through the Disability Access Service), and rides designed with a wide age range in mind. Grandparents who would never survive a day at a typical theme park can genuinely have a wonderful time at Disney, because the pacing can be managed, the transportation is easy, and there are beautiful, calm areas to sit and people-watch or enjoy a meal while the kids do something more intense.

The key is not trying to do everything. A day at EPCOT's World Showcase, walking slowly between the country pavilions and having lunch in France, suits a multi-generational group far better than a full-throttle day at Magic Kingdom trying to hit every ride.

All-Inclusive Resorts (Beaches in particular)

An all-inclusive resort solves a lot of multi-generational logistics. Everything is in one place. Grandparents don't have to coordinate transportation or worry about paying separately for meals. There's usually something for every pace of traveler — the pool, the beach, low-key water sports, nightly entertainment. Beaches Turks & Caicos in particular has a variety of programming that works across generations, from the kids clubs to the adult beach to the restaurants.

The lack of required activity is actually a feature for older generations who just want to sit on a beautiful beach with a drink and watch their grandchildren play in the water. That's not boring — that's perfect.

Cruises

Cruises are arguably the best multi-generational vacation format that exists. You unpack once. You sail to multiple destinations. The ship has programming for kids, teens, and adults, all happening simultaneously. Grandparents can attend the shows, enjoy the pools, have quiet dinners, and join the family for excursions when they feel up to it. Teens can use the teen club and have their own social experience. Little kids can do the kids club. Everyone comes back together for meals and certain activities, but nobody is stuck doing something that doesn't work for their age or energy level.

The Cruise Advantage

On a cruise, if grandparents are tired, they stay on the ship for a port day. That doesn't ruin anyone else's trip — everyone else goes on the shore excursion. The flexibility is built in.

Think About the Pace

Multi-generational trips require a slower pace than a trip with just adults or just one family unit. This is not a criticism — it's just reality, and designing your itinerary around it makes for a better trip.

Build in rest time every day. Don't plan activities back-to-back from morning to night. Leave room for spontaneous moments — a spontaneous ice cream stop, sitting together by the pool for an hour, a long dinner where nobody is rushing to the next thing. The most meaningful moments on these trips are often the unscheduled ones.

Plan for the possibility that not everyone will do everything together. Some activities will be for the whole group. Others will be for the younger, more active members while the grandparents rest. That's not a failure of the trip — it's a healthy acknowledgment that different people have different energy levels and interests, and that's okay.

Accessibility Planning

If you have grandparents or other family members with mobility limitations, accessibility needs to be a central planning consideration — not an afterthought.

  • At Walt Disney World® Resort: Request a first-floor room close to transportation if possible. Look into Disney's Disability Access Service (DAS), which allows guests with disabilities to avoid the standby queue. Rent a wheelchair or ECV (electric scooter) if needed — they're available to rent inside the parks.
  • On a cruise: Book an accessible stateroom early — they go fast. Make sure the ship's port stops include tender-free docking (where the ship docks directly and you walk off, rather than taking a small boat to shore), as tendering can be challenging for guests with mobility issues.
  • At an all-inclusive resort: Ask specifically about accessible room options, pool access, and beach wheelchair availability when booking.

The Budget Question

Money is the most delicate subject in multi-generational trip planning, and ignoring it leads to resentment. Have the conversation directly. Some families split costs evenly. Others have one generation covering a larger share. Some families pay for grandparents as a gift. Whatever the arrangement, being explicit about it upfront prevents misunderstandings later.

All-inclusive resorts and cruises are often the easiest formats for shared costs because the price is clearer upfront — you know what's covered and what isn't. Multiple separate hotel bookings in different cities with separate restaurant bills create more complexity and more potential for awkward conversations.

The Multi-Generational Secret

The families who have the best multi-generational trips aren't the ones who found the perfect destination. They're the ones who communicated honestly, planned with flexibility, and let go of the idea that every single moment needs to be all together. The goal is quality shared time — not quantity of shared activities. Build in some both/and moments and some individual moments, and the together time becomes even more meaningful.

My Best Destinations for Multi-Generational Trips

Based on the trips I've helped plan, these are the destinations that consistently work well for multi-generational family travel:

  • Walt Disney World® Resort — especially with grandparents who appreciate the thoughtful design and accessibility
  • Beaches Turks & Caicos — for the all-inclusive simplicity, the beautiful setting, and the range of activities
  • Disney Cruise Line — for the flexibility of cruise format combined with Disney's family-focused experience
  • Royal Caribbean — especially on the larger ships with adults-only areas, accessible staterooms, and a huge range of activity options
